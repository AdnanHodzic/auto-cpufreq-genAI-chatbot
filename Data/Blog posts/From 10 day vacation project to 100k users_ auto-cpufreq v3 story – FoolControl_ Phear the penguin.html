From 10 day vacation project to 100k users: auto-cpufreq v3 story

Adnan Hodzic January 25, 2026
0 Comments

How open source community and yes, even AI (Gemini) helped shape the journey to auto-cpufreq v3 release

Prefer watching over reading? Watch the full story YouTube:

From a Vacation Project to 100k Users

Today in tech, and life, we embrace failure and lessons we learn from it, which is good. But we’re focusing on failure aspect so much that once the opposite happens and you succeed, you don’t know what to do.

That’s what happened with auto-cpufreq, what started as a 10 day New Year’s vacation project 6 years ago is today an ultimate Linux performance & power optimizer with a life of its own:

  • 7300+ GitHub stars
  • 116 contributors
  • estimate of 100k users world wide

It all started with an idea I had for years, but never had time to work on, then New Years vacation was coming up, my 1st kid was supposed to be born in couple of months, and I thought if I don’t do this now, it’ll never be done. So I got to work, and frantically coded day and night and in less then 10 days, at end of my vacation released auto-cpufreq.

Idea immediately took off and got a great following on social media, it was covered on Youtube and various other news sites. At one point it was even trending as number 1 on Hacker News.

Even today, it’s great to see auto-cpufreq v3.0.0 release was feature on sites like: XDA Developers, Phoronix, It’s FOSS and even auto-cpufreq v3 release post on Reddit was seen by over 75k people and was trending as number 1 on Linux subreddit.

How you get to this point can’t just be based on hype, from the very first project release announcement, users were suggesting and requesting numerous features and fixes. Before first contributors showed up, in this process I put hundreds of hours of my own free time to work on development of what was suggested. Simple Python script immediately turned into a real Linux app and tool

Why do you need auto-cpufreq?

I might be bit biased when I say this, but even after all these years after it’s initial release, but auto-cpufreq is the best tool for the purpose it was built for originally. Which is automatic CPU speed and power optimization for Linux.

Without it watching Youtube in 4k will still be a choppy experience with high CPU temperatures, and I’ll get double of battery life I get with GNOME Power profiles without having to manually switching between performance and powersave profiles.

For new users and reminder for existing ones. After installing auto-cpufreq using recommended install method with auto-cpufreq-installer.

After the installation is complete, for CLI you can see list of available options by running:
auto-cpufreq --help

You can read more about what each mode does on auto-cpufreq project ReadMe modes and options section.

For GUI simply run auto-cpufreq app from listed installed applications and follow on screen instructions.

Please note that while auto-cpufreq will make its decision automatically based on various factors, if CPU usage is high and it detects you need more performance it will change governor to performance or will enable turbo, even if on battery. If it detects you don’t need performance, it’ll will give you a max battery life.

Besides that, you have numerous configuration options if you would like to configure things yourself, instead of it deciding everything for you automatically. Like forcing a governor, or using a configuration file which allows you to configure numerous things to your own liking.

Here you can configure how auto-cpufreq works on battery power or while it’s plugged and you can even configure it to never go below certain CPU threshold like I did above.

In case you’re using a config file, you’ll see this as soon as you run: auto-cpufreq --stats

The Power of the Crowd: Community building with Open Source

I immediately knew I won’t be able to do all this myself. Being involved in open source since an early age, I started accepting external contributions and I was actively encouraging people to make contributions themselves and crediting them for their work as part of each release.

Which grew into a whole network and community of contributors, and I’m very thankful to all 116 individuals who contributed to auto-cpufreq as without them, this project wouldn’t be where it is today.

Which can only happen with open source software, as besides few donations, none of us are getting paid for any of this, everyone is doing this in their own free time next to daily jobs and lives, driven by desire to solve a real problem, learn something along the way and build a tool that actually makes the Linux experience better for everyone.

With number of contributors growing, it’s important to have rigorous code testing and review process and not merge everything. In this process numerous PR (pull requests) were submitted that were never merged.

As they simply lacked on quality or didn’t fit the overall vision. Each time I decided to close one eye or missed something, and merged something that shouldn’t be merged, it had consequences that were addressed later for months. Of course, you also don’t want to chase contributors away, so it’s a very delicate balancing act.

Scaling with AI: The Gemini Support Agent

My focus also shifted with the project as it got bigger, development was also superseded by numerous support requests and triage. Answering various questions on what’s possible and available. Or simply answering a question by linking it to a peace of documentation or something that was mentioned in releases.

But this was taking enormous amount of my time, as being the person who knows most about the project turned me into an answering machine.

There’s an interesting paradox, while people are interested in making code contributions and developing new features and improvements, rare few are also interested in actively supporting the feature they developed. Of course, kudos to the outliers.

That’s why as part of each PR I also made it a standard procedure and request to add documentation for developed features and improvements, which helped drastically in long term.

With the rise of Artificial Intelligence throughout the tech landscape and looking at some possible solutions and AI applications, I was inspired to develop a dedicated auto-cpufreq genAl chatbot as a solution to all above mentioned challenges.

Hence, “How I replaced myself with a genAI chatbot using Gemini” project was born.

Which revolves around creating a RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) based on numerous auto-cpufreq data sources which have all the necessary data, from these Youtube videos, to Github issues, Reddit posts and etc … and the creating genAI chatpot with Vertex AI Conversational Agents in Google Cloud to provide users with necessary answers to their questions and support request.

In the end, this project turned out to be an amazing success, it was featured on official Google blog, I even gave a talk about at it Google Amsterdam office as part of Google /dev/cloud day event/conference.

Since its release, when creating a new issue users are encouraged to use it to use auto-cpufreq genAI chatbot to see if their question was already answered and issue resolved. Number of newly created issues and questions has drastically decreased while auto-cpufreq users continue to increase.

Which is a clear sign that it auto-cpufreq genAI chatbot does its job.

The Road to v3: Key Feature Highlights

So let’s talk about what’s new in auto-cpufreq v3, since last time I made a similar video was back in auto-cpufreq v2.0 release & demo Youtube video:

Since each release has too many fixes and improvements to just name a few I’ll only mention some of the features for each release since v2.0.0.

v2.1.0

  • If you’re running auto-cpufreq in GUI, if there’s a new update you’ll be notified about it
  • Behind the scenes, project moved to pyproject.toml using Poetry as the backend, which modernized the build process and simplified packaging to align with current Python standards.

v2.2.0

  • Adding support for configuring or overriding “Energy Performance Preference (EPP)”: allows hardware to make millisecond-level voltage decisions, unlocking significantly better performance-per-watt and solving battery drain on modern Intel and AMD laptops.
  • Add battery charge thresholds limit: which for example allows you to stop charging at 80%, and this feature was added way before it was added to major projects and desktop environments like GNOME.

v2.3.0

  • Add “conservation mode” for Lenovo laptops
  • Rework config and reload config on file change, creation, deletion is one of my favorite features, as if you’re using auto-cpufreq.conf file, after making changes to the file they will be automatically and immediately applied, instead of having to restart auto-cpufreq systemd service or rebooting the computer.

v2.4.0

I think this was one of the biggest releases with most changes:

  • Add support for setting “Platform Profile”: allowing to overriding and set “Platform Profile” helped avoid throttling issues in high performance mode on charging profile (like throttling due to lap-mode on ThinkPads) and save some power in battery mode if firmware supports it.
  • Support was dinit (Atrix) init system was added, a great alternative to those not using systemd

v2.5.0

  • Add EPB (Energy Performance Bias) settings support: giving users more control over how their Intel CPU (intel_pstate driver) balances performance and power efficiency

v2.6.0

  • Implement dedicated functionality to turn Bluetooth on/off at boot: so this is now a separate flag as in past bluetooth would be automatically disabled on boot with auto-cpufreq daemon install.
  • Enhance System Monitoring with a TUI: was a major overhaul of the auto-cpufreq CLI, transforming raw text into a dynamic dashboard that makes monitoring system statistics much more intuitive.

v3.0.0

Which leads us to our latest release the big v3

  • Which besides having this option in auto-cpufreq conf file, now “allows you to override the CPU turbo setting via CLI and GUI”
  • Allow users to specify battery device in config: which ensures auto-cpufreq works flawlessly on laptops with non-standard battery names or dual-battery setups where automatic detection might fail.

Since v3 release, which was just over a week, I’ve already drafted release for v3.1 as there are already 2 new contributors and one of them already created a great new feature which gives you ability to see monitoring mode in GUI.

One thing that wasn’t listed anywhere but happened right after the v2 release, was the creation of the auto-cpufreq Discord community.

In meantime, as always, we’re looking for developers and co-maintainers in case you’re interested in shaping the future of this project.

Future plans and the Power of Shared Vision

In closing, this has been an incredible journey. From starting this project as a solo developer to now being the “tech lead” for a global project with some 100k users has been an unique experience to say the least.

As for where we go next? The project has truly taken on a life of its own, so I’ll continue to go with its flow.

I’ve been thinking a lot about further AI integration. I keep thinking how great it would be to integrate something like gemini-cli, moving away from pre-set logic and allowing the system to actually reason about power and performance decisions, or help users troubleshoot issues live through natural language. The idea is still forming, but it could be an interesting direction to explore 🙂

In the end, today I hear a lot of folks saying “open source being dead” and that’s because most of big projects are sponsored by big companies, also means they are pretty much run by those same companies.

While I would love to see corporate sponsorship for this project, especially because I believe auto-cpufreq could significantly aid various companies’ sustainability efforts by reducing power consumption in data centers, this project proves that sponsorship isn’t a requirement for success.

auto-cpufreq is a testament to what can be achieved when people share the same vision as you. It proves that open source doesn’t need corporate sponsorship to be successful. It’s a perfect example of how powerful open source community can be.

So if, you have an idea of your own … just go for it

Until next time
